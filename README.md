## The concept

### What is a learning and employment record?

You already know from ICB-F01 that a digital badge or credential describes one achievement. It says who earned it, who issued it and what it stands for. A single credential is useful, but a person's learning and working life is made of many pieces.

A **learning and employment record (LER)** is a digital record that holds many pieces of a person's learning and work history together. It can include:

- courses and degrees,
- digital badges and micro-credentials,
- professional licences and memberships,
- work experience and job roles,
- skills, with evidence behind them,
- non-formal learning such as workshops and volunteering.

Two ideas matter most. First, the record is **comprehensive**: it covers more than one achievement and more than one issuer. Second, the record is **learner-held**: the person keeps it, for example in a digital wallet, and decides what to share, with whom and when.

### Why records, and why now?

People change jobs, move between countries and learn in many places. Each issuer holds its own data in its own system. A learner who wants to show their full story has to collect documents from many sources and send them one by one.

A record changes the experience. The learner sees their whole history in one place. An employer, a university or an agency can receive a selected view of it, with proof that each part comes from its issuer. For the learner, this is about **value** (the record helps them reach opportunities) and **experience** (sharing is simple and under their control).

A record also supports skills-based practices. When achievements are described with skills and evidence, an employer or admissions officer can look at what a person has done, not only at the title of a course.

### Records and single credentials

A record does not replace single credentials. Each credential keeps its own issuer, its own evidence and its own verification. The record is the container and the structure around them. Think of a folder that holds verified documents, with an index that explains how they relate.

**Worked example: A college and a professional body**

*University, college or training provider.* A regional college runs a two-year health care assistant programme. Today a graduate receives a PDF certificate, a transcript and three short course badges. In a record-based approach, the college issues all of these as verifiable credentials, and the graduate stores them in a wallet. The graduate also adds a placement completion statement from a hospital.

*Professional body, sector association or public agency.* A national association for care workers issues a membership credential and a continuing development log. The graduate adds both to the same wallet. When applying for a job, the graduate sends the employer one selected view: the college award, the placement statement and the membership. The employer can check each part with its issuer. All numbers and details here are illustrative.

**Practice activity: Map your own record**

List five achievements from your own education and work. For each one, write down who issued it, in what form you hold it (paper, PDF, email, badge) and how hard it would be to share with a new employer today. Then mark which ones could sit in one learner-held record. Keep the list for the last module.

## CLR 2.0

### The standard in one picture

**CLR 2.0** is the Comprehensive Learner Record standard, version 2.0. It is a current, established specification, published by 1EdTech and designed to work together with Open Badges 3.0. Both specifications build on the W3C Verifiable Credentials data model. <!-- VERIFY: Confirm that 1EdTech is the publisher of CLR 2.0 and Open Badges 3.0 and that both align with the W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model before publishing. -->

You do not need to read the technical text to understand the idea. A CLR is a verifiable credential that **contains other credentials and descriptions of learning and work**. It is a structured wrapper. Inside it, the learner's achievements are described in a shared data model so that other systems can read them.

### Main building blocks

Without going into technical detail, you should recognise these ideas.

- **The CLR credential.** The outer credential. It is issued to the learner and holds the contents.
- **Learner and issuer.** Every credential names who it is about and who issued it. This lets a receiver check where it comes from.
- **Achievements.** Descriptions of what was achieved: a course, a degree, a badge, a role. Each has a name, a description and often criteria.
- **Results and evidence.** An achievement can carry results, such as a grade or outcome, and evidence, such as a link to work or an assessment record.
- **Skills and alignments.** An achievement can point to skills or frameworks, so that systems can compare it with job requirements or other records.
- **Associations.** Links that show how items relate, for example that a course is part of a degree, or that a badge is a component of a programme.
- **Proof.** A digital signature or similar mechanism allows a receiver to check that the content has not been changed and who issued it.

### Relationship to Open Badges 3.0

Open Badges 3.0 describes a single achievement credential. CLR 2.0 describes a set of achievements and their relationships. The two share a data model for achievements. This means an Open Badges 3.0 credential can be included in a CLR, and tools that understand one can often work with the other.

A practical point for anyone selecting tools: both specifications are established, but support in platforms is still uneven. Some tools issue but cannot display. Some tools display but do not verify. Before you choose a tool, check whether it holds a **conformance certification** for the version of the standard you need. Do not rely on a product description alone.

**Worked example: A university and a public agency**

*University, college or training provider.* A university registry wants to give graduates a record at the end of their studies. It decides to issue a CLR 2.0 credential that contains the degree award, each module as an achievement with its result, and two Open Badges 3.0 credentials for extra-curricular skills. A registry officer checks that the issuing platform holds conformance certification for CLR 2.0 and that a test record opens correctly in two different wallets.

*Professional body, sector association or public agency.* A public agency for vocational training issues attendance and assessment achievements for adult learners. It uses the same data model, so a learner can add the agency achievements to the same wallet that holds university credentials. Associations in the data model link each module to the qualification it counts towards. All details are illustrative.

**Practice activity: Read a record structure**

Imagine a record with one qualification, three modules and one badge. Sketch it on paper as boxes. Mark the outer credential, the learner, the issuer, the achievements and the associations. Then write one sentence for each box saying what a receiving system could do with that information.

### The United States

In the United States, the idea of an LER has been driven by a mix of education institutions, employers, standards bodies and public programmes. Interest comes from the wish to describe skills gained in many places and to connect learning to hiring and advancement. Many projects take the form of pilots and networks of partners who agree to exchange records.

Common features you will meet in descriptions of this approach:

- a strong link between records and skills-based hiring,
- use of CLR 2.0 and Open Badges 3.0 as data standards,
- partnerships between employers, colleges and technology providers,
- attention to how records can support career pathways.

<!-- VERIFY: Add specific US initiatives, programmes or bodies only after confirming current names, scope and status with official sources. -->

### Europe

In Europe, the starting point is different. There are established public frameworks for qualifications and a policy interest in recognition across borders. Work on digital credentials is often connected to public infrastructure, shared rules and the aim of making qualifications easy to recognise and verify between countries and sectors.

Common features you will meet in descriptions of this approach:

- links between credentials and national or European qualification frameworks,
- emphasis on recognition, mobility and trust in public systems,
- shared specifications and infrastructure that many institutions can use,
- attention to privacy and learner control under European rules.

The European picture is varied. Countries and institutions differ in how far they have moved from pilots to everyday use, and LERs as a full concept are less advanced than in the United States.

### What the regions share

Despite different routes, the two regions share important ideas. Both aim for records that are verifiable and learner-held. Both depend on common data models so that systems can read each other's records. Both face the same practical questions: how to describe skills consistently, how to protect personal data and how to make wallets and platforms work together.

For a person who selects standards for a programme, this means the regional context matters but the technical core is similar. A tool that conforms to CLR 2.0 and Open Badges 3.0 can serve either context. What changes is the surrounding framework: which qualification frameworks you link to, which laws apply and which partners you work with.

**Worked example: A training provider and a public agency in different regions**

*University, college or training provider.* A private training provider works with learners in both the United States and a European country. It issues the same CLR 2.0 structure for both. For US employers it adds skills alignments to job-related frameworks. For the European learners it also adds a link to the relevant national qualification level where one applies. The data model is the same, and the alignments differ by audience.

*Professional body, sector association or public agency.* A public agency in a European country plans to accept credentials from foreign providers. Its staff compare how the provider's record describes level, learning outcomes and assessment with the agency's own framework. They decide that the record gives enough information for a first review, and they keep a manual check for unclear cases. All details are illustrative.

## Implications

### What a comprehensive record adds

A single credential answers one question: did this person achieve this, according to this issuer? A comprehensive record can answer wider questions. This is the core of the third learning outcome.

A comprehensive record adds context, connection, completeness and control to what single credentials already offer.

**Completeness.** A record brings together achievements from many issuers and settings. The receiver sees the whole picture instead of a single piece. This includes learning that sits outside formal education.

**Context.** Items in a record can show how they relate: a module belongs to a qualification, a badge belongs to a programme, a role used a skill. Associations and alignments give meaning that a lone credential cannot give.

**Connection.** Because records use shared data models, systems can read them. An admissions system, a recruitment tool and a skills platform can all use the same record. This reduces retyping and reduces errors.

**Control.** The learner holds the record and chooses what to share. A learner can send a selected view for one purpose and keep the rest private. This links back to the quality focus on **Value** (the record helps the learner reach opportunities) and **Experience** (sharing is simple and respects the learner's choices).

### What a record does not add

It is useful to be clear about limits.

- A record does not make a weak credential strong. Each item is only as trustworthy as its issuer and its assessment.
- A record does not prove that a person can do a job. It shows what an issuer has stated about learning or experience. Employers still judge fit for a role.
- A record does not remove the need for good data. If skills are described inconsistently, systems will struggle to compare them.
- A record does not solve interoperability by itself. Platform support is uneven, so testing and conformance checks remain necessary.

### Questions to ask as a decision maker

If you are selecting standards and profiles for a programme, keep a short list of questions in mind.

1. What problem would a record solve for our learners or members?
2. Which of our credentials would go in it, and who issues them?
3. Which standards and versions do our partners and tools support?
4. Do our tools hold conformance certification for those versions?
5. How will learners store, view and share their record?
6. What data protection rules apply to the content?
7. How will we explain the record to employers, admissions staff and learners?

These questions connect the knowledge in this course to the skill described at level L3: selecting standards and profiles, mapping data models and resolving interoperability issues. In this course you build the understanding behind those decisions. Practising the decisions themselves belongs to a later stage.

**Worked example: A college and a sector association**

*University, college or training provider.* A college registry lists the credentials it issues: diplomas, short course certificates, digital badges and work placement confirmations. Registry staff group them by learner journey and decide which belong in a record. They find that placement confirmations come from employers and are not yet digital. They note this as a gap and agree to ask two partner employers to issue them in a compatible form.

*Professional body, sector association or public agency.* A sector association issues membership grades and a yearly continuing development statement. It considers adding both to learners' records. Staff ask what a member gains: easier proof for employers, one place for all statements and control over sharing. They also note a risk: members may be confused if wallets display the items differently. The association plans a short guide for members. All details are illustrative.

**Practice activity: Return to your list**

Take the list you made in the first module. For each item, write one sentence on what it would add if it sat in a record with the others: completeness, context, connection or control. Then write one thing the record would not fix.
